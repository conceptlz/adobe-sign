    Signature levels: SES, AES and QES

    Not every signature carries the same legal weight. This page explains the three levels that European law (and laws modelled on it, such as the UAE's) recognises, what each one means technically inside Acrobat Sign, and how to request a level per project, per agreement or per signer. It also shows how to prove afterwards which level each person actually reached.

    The short version

    The SDK defaults to SES, Acrobat Sign's normal e-signature. You can raise the level for a whole project, for one agreement, or for one signer. AES and QES are the same API request; what makes a signature qualified is the trust service provider that issues the signer's certificate, which is configured on your Acrobat Sign account, not in code.

    SES

    Simple electronic signature

    The signer types, draws or clicks. Adobe seals the finished PDF with its own certificate and records an audit trail.

    Good for most commercial paperwork: NDAs, order forms, HR acknowledgements.

    AES

    Advanced electronic signature

    The signer signs with a digital certificate issued to them by a cloud signature provider. Their own certificate is embedded in the PDF.

    For higher-value or regulated agreements where you want the signature bound to an identified person.

    QES

    Qualified electronic signature

    An AES made with a qualified certificate from a qualified trust service provider, on a qualified signature creation device.

    Treated like a handwritten signature in the EU. Needed where a law or contract asks for it.

    This page is orientation, not legal advice

    It summarises how the frameworks are generally understood, so that developers and technical leads can have an informed conversation. Whether a given signature satisfies a requirement depends on the jurisdiction, the type of document and the facts. Confirm your choice of level with your own counsel or compliance team. The SDK requests a level and reports what happened; it does not decide what the law requires.

    What eIDAS says

    eIDAS is the European Union's regulation on electronic identification and trust services, Regulation (EU) No 910/2014. It has applied across the EU since 2016 and was amended by Regulation (EU) 2024/1183, often called "eIDAS 2". Because it is a regulation rather than a directive, the rules are the same in every member state, and the EEA countries follow it too. It defines three levels of electronic signature, each building on the one below.

    Simple electronic signature (SES)

    The regulation's baseline definition is broad: data in electronic form that is attached to, or logically associated with, other electronic data and that the signatory uses to sign. A typed name, a drawn squiggle, a scanned image or a click on "I agree" can all qualify.

    The important legal point is the non-discrimination rule: a court may not refuse an electronic signature as evidence, or deny it legal effect, just because it is electronic or because it isn't qualified. An SES is valid. What it lacks is a built-in presumption: if the signer later says "that wasn't me", the party relying on the signature has to prove otherwise, using evidence such as Acrobat Sign's audit report (email address, IP address, timestamps, identity checks).

    Advanced electronic signature (AES)

    An advanced signature is an electronic signature that meets four requirements:

    1. Uniquely linked to the signatory

      The signature belongs to one person and no one else.

    2. Capable of identifying the signatory

      From the signature you can tell who signed, typically because the certificate names them.

    3. Created under the signatory's sole control

      The signing key is something only the signer can use, with a high level of confidence; for cloud signatures that means the provider authenticates the signer before each use.

    4. Linked to the signed data so that changes are detectable

      Alter a single byte of the document afterwards and the signature no longer validates.

    In practice these requirements point towards public-key cryptography: a certificate issued to the signer, and a signature computed over a hash of the document. That is exactly what Acrobat Sign produces when you ask for a digital signature.

    Qualified electronic signature (QES)

    A qualified signature is an advanced signature with two extra ingredients: it is based on a qualified certificate for electronic signatures, and it is created by a qualified signature creation device (QSCD). Qualified certificates may only be issued by a qualified trust service provider (QTSP): a provider that has been audited against the regulation's requirements, granted qualified status by a national supervisory body and listed on that country's trusted list. Before issuing a qualified certificate, the QTSP must verify the person's identity to a high standard (in person, by video identification, with a national eID, and so on).

    The QSCD used to be a smart card or USB token. Today it is usually a certified hardware security module run by the QTSP, which holds the signer's key and lets them use it remotely after strong authentication. This "remote QSCD" model is what makes QES possible from a browser, and it is how cloud providers integrated with Acrobat Sign work.

    The legal effect is the strongest the regulation offers: a QES has the equivalent legal effect of a handwritten signature, and a QES based on a qualified certificate issued in one member state must be recognised as a QES in all the others.

    Trusted lists

    Each member state publishes a trusted list of its QTSPs and their qualified services, and the European Commission publishes the list of those lists. Validation software uses them to decide whether a certificate was qualified at the moment of signing. You can browse them through the Commission's trusted list browser. When this page says a provider is "qualified", it means the provider and the specific service used appear on a trusted list.

    What changed with Regulation (EU) 2024/1183

    The 2024 amendment keeps the three signature levels as they were. Its headline change is the European Digital Identity Wallet: member states must offer citizens a wallet that holds identity data and attestations, and the wallets are expected to let people create qualified electronic signatures. It also adds new trust services such as electronic attestations of attributes and electronic ledgers. For an integration built on Acrobat Sign, the practical effect so far is that more people may already hold a qualified signing means, and more providers may appear as options. The way you request levels through the API is unchanged.

    How the UAE recognises electronic signatures

    The UAE's main federal law is Federal Decree-Law No. 46 of 2021 on electronic transactions and trust services, which replaced the earlier federal e-commerce law. In broad terms:

    • Electronic signatures and electronic records are recognised, and a signature is not refused legal effect only because it is electronic.
    • The law describes tiers that broadly mirror eIDAS: an ordinary electronic signature, a trusted (reliable) electronic signature, and a qualified electronic signature backed by a qualified certificate from a licensed provider. The stronger tiers carry stronger presumptions, and qualified signatures are generally given the same force as handwritten ones.
    • The Telecommunications and Digital Government Regulatory Authority (TDRA) supervises trust services and licenses trust service providers. There are also arrangements for recognising foreign trust services.
    • UAE PASS, the national digital identity, offers digital signing to residents and citizens.
    • Some transactions are excluded from electronic execution or need extra formalities. Historically these have included personal-status matters, wills, certain real-estate dealings and documents that must be notarised. Check the current list for your document type.
    • Financial free zones such as the DIFC and ADGM have their own legislation on electronic transactions, which can differ from the federal rules.

    Confirm UAE specifics locally

    Implementing regulations and the list of licensed providers change over time, and the answer can depend on the emirate, the free zone and the counterparty (government entities often have their own requirements). Treat this section as a map of the questions to ask, and get the answers from counsel who practise in the UAE.

    Which level do I need?

    Start from the highest requirement anyone places on the document, then step down. Most commercial agreements are fine with SES plus a sensible identity check. AES is a business choice for higher assurance. QES is usually driven by a specific legal or contractual requirement.

    Typical documentLevel people usually chooseWhy
    NDA, order form, statement of work, HR policy acknowledgementSESLow dispute risk; the audit report is ample evidence.
    Sales contract or lease with a known customerSES with Email OTP or SMSCheap extra assurance that the right person opened it.
    Loan, guarantee, high-value or cross-border contractAESSignature bound to an identified person; tamper-evident.
    Documents where a statute or regulator asks for a qualified signature, or where a counterparty insists on "handwritten-equivalent" signaturesQESEquivalent legal effect to a handwritten signature in the EU.

    These are common patterns, not rules. Your counsel decides; the SDK makes any of the three a one-line change.

    How Acrobat Sign implements each level

    Acrobat Sign has a single switch for this, isDigitalSignatureRequired, set per participant set. The SDK translates levels into that switch:

    LevelWhat the SDK sendsWhat the signer doesWhat ends up in the PDF
    SESnothing extra (Acrobat Sign's default)types, draws or uploads a signature imageAdobe's certification seal over the whole document
    AES"isDigitalSignatureRequired": true on the signer's setpicks a cloud signature provider, authenticates with it, signs with their certificateAdobe's seal plus the signer's own certificate as an embedded PDF signature
    QESthe same as AESthe same, with a qualified providerthe same, with a qualified certificate

    The API cannot express "qualified": Adobe offers a digital signature and lets the account decide which providers are available. That is why the SDK keeps QES as a separate value at all: it records your intent, so you can check the result against it after signing.

    Choosing a level in code

    Levels come from Conceptlz\AdobeSign\Enum\SignatureLevel, a backed enum with the cases SES, AES and QES. Two helper methods make comparisons easy: requiresDigitalSignature() is true for AES and QES, and satisfies(SignatureLevel $required) is true when a level is at least as strong as the one required (QES satisfies AES, AES satisfies SES).

    A project-wide default

    Set ClientOptions::$defaultSignatureLevel once and every agreement built with AgreementInfo uses it unless it says otherwise:

    The default is SignatureLevel::SES, which sends nothing extra and keeps Acrobat Sign's normal behaviour. The default applies only to agreements built with the AgreementInfo builder: raw arrays and generated AgreementCreationInfo models are sent exactly as you wrote them.

    Per agreement

    AgreementInfo::signatureLevel() sets the level for every signer in the agreement, overriding the client default:

    use Conceptlz\AdobeSign\Builder\AgreementInfo;
use Conceptlz\AdobeSign\Enum\SignatureLevel;

$agreementId = $sign->agreements()->create(
    AgreementInfo::create('Employment contract')
        ->file($documentId)
        ->signers('employee@example.com', 'hr-director@example.com')
        ->signatureLevel(SignatureLevel::QES),
);

    Per signer, in either direction

    ParticipantSetInfo::signatureLevel() overrides the agreement's level for one participant set. It works both ways: raise one signer above the rest, or let one signer sign more simply than the rest. Here is one agreement where the borrower signs with AES and the witness with SES:

    The builder turns that into a payload where only the first set asks for a digital signature:

    "participantSetsInfo": [
  {
    "role": "SIGNER",
    "order": 1,
    "memberInfos": [{ "email": "borrower@example.com", "name": "Borrower" }],
    "isDigitalSignatureRequired": true
  },
  {
    "role": "SIGNER",
    "order": 2,
    "memberInfos": [{ "email": "witness@example.com" }]
  }
]

    The opposite direction works the same way: a QES agreement with one internal signer who only needs SES.

    AgreementInfo::create('Share purchase agreement')
    ->file($documentId)
    ->signatureLevel(SignatureLevel::QES)                                  // everyone…
    ->participantSet(ParticipantSetInfo::signer('buyer@example.com'))
    ->participantSet(ParticipantSetInfo::signer('seller@example.com'))
    ->participantSet(
        ParticipantSetInfo::signer('company-secretary@example.com')
            ->signatureLevel(SignatureLevel::SES),                          // …except this signer
    );

    Which level wins

    SourceApplies toPrecedence
    ParticipantSetInfo::signatureLevel()that participant sethighest
    AgreementInfo::signatureLevel()every signer set without its own levelmiddle
    ClientOptions::$defaultSignatureLevelagreements whose builder sets no levellowest

    Only sets with the SIGNER role ever receive a level. Approvers, acceptors, form fillers, certified recipients and delegates don't sign, so the agreement-level and project-level settings skip them. You can read back what you set with AgreementInfo::getSignatureLevel() and ParticipantSetInfo::getSignatureLevel().

    Validation before anything is sent

    The builders refuse combinations that can't work and throw Conceptlz\AdobeSign\Exception\InvalidArgumentException before any request is made, so a requested level is never dropped without you knowing:

    You do thisThe SDK says
    Ask for AES or QES on an agreement with signatureType(SignatureType::WRITTEN) AES and QES need electronic signatures; this agreement uses signatureType WRITTEN.
    Call AgreementInfo::signatureLevel() with AES or QES on an agreement that has no SIGNER set (only approvers, say) AES was requested but the agreement has no signer to apply it to.
    Call signatureLevel(SignatureLevel::AES) on an approver set A APPROVER set does not sign, so it cannot be held to AES.

    Two details are easy to miss:

    • The "no signer" rule applies only to a level set explicitly on the agreement. A project default of AES does not break approval-only agreements; it simply has no one to apply to.
    • The WRITTEN rule does include the project default. If your client defaults to AES and you need a written (wet-ink) agreement, set ->signatureLevel(SignatureLevel::SES) on it explicitly.

    Adobe can also refuse a digital-signature request for account reasons: the spec lists DIGITAL_SIGNATURE_ENFORCEMENT_PER_PARTICIPANT_NOT_ALLOWED and ..._DISABLED as possible 403 answers to POST /agreements. These arrive as an ApiException with that $errorCode, and are fixed in Acrobat Sign admin, not in code. See Errors & exceptions.

    What the signer experiences

    For an SES, nothing changes: the signer opens the email, clicks through to the document and types or draws a signature. For AES and QES, the signing step looks different:

    1. The usual email

      The invitation looks the same as for any agreement. If you added an identity check, that happens first.

    2. A provider dropdown

      When the signer clicks the signature field, Acrobat Sign asks them to sign with a digital ID and shows a dropdown of the cloud signature providers (trust service providers) that your account has enabled for their region.

    3. Authenticate with the provider

      The signer is sent to the chosen provider and logs in with whatever that provider requires, for example a username, a PIN and a one-time code. Someone without an identity at that provider has to enrol first, which can include the provider's own identity proofing.

    4. Sign with a cloud certificate

      The provider uses the certificate it holds for the signer to sign the document hash, and hands the signature back to Acrobat Sign. The signer never installs anything.

    5. Back in Acrobat Sign

      Adobe embeds the signature in the PDF, logs a DIGSIGNED event and moves the agreement to the next step in the signing order.

    What a real AES looked like

    When the SDK was tested against a real account, the signer picked Intesi Group Cloud Signature from the dropdown and signed with a test certificate issued by Intesi Group Advanced Cloud Signature CA Test. That is an advanced certificate, not a qualified one, so the result was an AES and not a QES. Adobe recorded the signature like this (trimmed): 

    {
  "type": "DIGSIGNED",
  "participantRole": "SIGNER",
  "signatureInputType": "TYPED",
  "digitalSignatureInfo": {
    "certificateIssuer": "Intesi Group Advanced Cloud Signature CA Test",
    "providerName": "Intesi Group Cloud Signature",
    "providerUrl": "https://services.time4mind.com/csc/v1/",
    "providerIp": "194.15.44.117"
  }
}

    Two things in that payload are worth knowing:

    • Adobe's spec names the provider fields cloudProviderName, cloudProviderUrl and cloudProviderIp; the live API sent providerName, providerUrl and providerIp. signatureEvidence() reads both spellings. The generated DigitalSignatureInfo model only has the spec's names, so if you read events yourself, look in the raw array too.
    • signatureInputType was TYPED even though the signature was digital: it describes how the visible signature appearance was made, not the cryptography. Use the event type and digitalSignatureInfo to tell digital signatures apart.

    Why QES depends on the provider

    Everything that makes a signature qualified lives at the provider: the qualified certificate, the identity proofing done before issuing it, and the certified device (the QSCD) that holds the key. Acrobat Sign orchestrates the signing, but the provider performs it. So the same API request yields an AES with one provider and a QES with another. Even one provider can offer both an advanced and a qualified service, as the test above shows.

    Setting an account up for QES

    1. Check the plan

      Digital signatures with cloud providers need an Acrobat Sign plan that includes them. Ask your Adobe representative; QTSPs also charge for their certificates and signatures.

    2. Enable digital signatures and add a qualified provider

      In Acrobat Sign admin, enable digital signatures and add a qualified trust service provider as a cloud signature provider. Restrict the list for the regions or groups that need QES, so signers can't pick a non-qualified option by mistake.

    3. Make sure signers can get a qualified certificate

      Each signer needs an identity with that provider. Tell them in the agreement message, because enrolment can take a few minutes the first time.

    4. Request QES in code and record the accepted issuers

      Use SignatureLevel::QES, and keep the certificate issuers your compliance team accepts as qualified in configuration, for the check after signing.

    The EU trusted lists show which providers and services are qualified. Several QTSPs advertise integrations with Acrobat Sign; confirm with the provider which of its services is qualified, and under which jurisdiction.

    The qualified-issuers list

    The SDK can see the certificate issuer of each digital signature, but it cannot know by itself whether that issuer is qualified. You tell it, with a list of issuer names you accept. Matching is case-insensitive and by substring, so 'TrustPro QTSP' matches an issuer called "TrustPro QTSP Qualified CA 1". 

    // Keep this in configuration so compliance can change it without a deploy.
$qualifiedIssuers = array_values(array_filter(array_map(
    'trim',
    explode(',', getenv('QUALIFIED_ISSUERS') ?: ''),
)));

    The bundled examples/live-lifecycle.php reads the same QUALIFIED_ISSUERS variable, and the Laravel bridge reads it from its qualified_issuers config key. Keep the names specific: a bare company name can match that company's advanced (non-qualified) CA too.

    Checking what was achieved

    Requesting a level is half the job. After signing, ask the SDK what each signer actually did: 

    agreements()->signatureEvidence(string $agreementId): SignatureEvidenceGET /agreements/{agreementId} + /members + /events

    Makes three calls (the agreement's status, its members and its events) and joins them per signer. A signer counts as signed when Adobe recorded a signing event for their email (ACTION_COMPLETED, ACTION_COMPLETED_HOSTED, ACTION_COMPLETED_OFFLINE or DIGSIGNED), and as digitally signed when that event is a DIGSIGNED event or carries a non-empty digitalSignatureInfo. Only SIGNER sets are included, in signing order. 

    use Conceptlz\AdobeSign\Enum\SignatureLevel;

$evidence = $sign->agreements()->signatureEvidence($agreementId);

if (!$evidence->allSigned()) {
    return; // try again after AGREEMENT_WORKFLOW_COMPLETED
}

if ($evidence->meets(SignatureLevel::QES, $qualifiedIssuers)) {
    $contract->markQualified();
} else {
    foreach ($evidence->shortfalls(SignatureLevel::QES, $qualifiedIssuers) as $problem) {
        $log->warning($problem);
        // "cfo@example.com signed digitally, but the certificate issuer "Intesi Group Advanced
        //  Cloud Signature CA Test" is not in your list of qualified issuers: AES, QES required."
    }
}

    SignatureEvidence

    MemberWhat it gives you
    $agreementId, $statusThe agreement and its status when you asked (e.g. SIGNED).
    $signersA list of SignerEvidence, sorted by signing order.
    allSigned(): boolTrue when every signer has signed (and there is at least one).
    meets(SignatureLevel $required, array $qualifiedIssuers = []): boolTrue when every signer reached $required or stronger.
    shortfalls(SignatureLevel $required, array $qualifiedIssuers = []): arrayHuman-readable reasons, one per signer who falls short; empty when meets() is true.
    lowestLevel(array $qualifiedIssuers = []): ?SignatureLevelThe weakest level any signer reached, or null while someone still has to sign.

    meets() and shortfalls() throw InvalidArgumentException when you check for QES with an empty issuer list, because without it no signature could ever be counted as qualified.

    SignerEvidence

    Property or methodMeaning
    $email, $nameThe signer (the name from the signing event when Adobe has one).
    $orderTheir step in the signing order.
    $signed, $signedAtWhether and when (ISO 8601) they signed.
    $signatureInputTypeHow the visible signature was made, e.g. TYPED or SCRIBBLE (drawn).
    $digitalSignatureRequiredWhether the agreement asked this signer for a digital signature (AES or QES requested).
    $digitallySignedWhether a certificate-based signature was actually applied.
    $certificateIssuerThe issuer of the signer's certificate, when digitally signed.
    $signingProviderThe cloud provider that produced the signature, when digitally signed.
    $verificationThe identity check required before signing: NONE, EMAIL_OTP, PHONE, KBA, GOV_ID, DIG_ID, …
    achievedLevel(array $qualifiedIssuers = []): ?SignatureLevelnull if not signed; SES for an e-signature; AES for a digital signature; QES when the issuer is in your list.
    issuedByAnyOf(array $issuers): boolWhether the certificate issuer matches any name in the list.

    Checking a mixed agreement

    meets() holds every signer to one level. In the loan example above the witness signed with SES on purpose, so $evidence->meets(SignatureLevel::AES) is false even though everything went to plan. For agreements that mix levels, check each signer against what you asked of them:

    How the SDK infers the level, and its limits

    • SES: a signing event without digital signature data.
    • AES: a DIGSIGNED event, or a signing event with digitalSignatureInfo.
    • QES: an AES whose certificateIssuer matches your list.

    Signers are matched by email. If a signer delegated to someone else, the delegate's signature is matched only when Adobe reports it under the original signer's email. Identity checks are reported in $verification but never change the inferred level.

    Formal validation of the PDF

    signatureEvidence() reads Adobe's records, which is the right tool for workflow decisions ("can we release the goods?"). For an authoritative statement, for example in a dispute, validate the signatures inside the signed PDF itself against the trusted lists, using validation software such as the EU's DSS demonstration service or Acrobat's signature panel. What is inside the signed PDF explains which signatures you will find there.

    Identity checks are a separate dimension

    Email OTP, SMS codes, knowledge-based questions, government ID scans and national eIDs control who can open the agreement. They make an SES much more convincing as evidence, and they are worth adding at any level, but they don't turn an SES into an AES. Only a certificate-based digital signature does that. Combine the two dimensions freely: 

    use Conceptlz\AdobeSign\Builder\{ParticipantInfo, ParticipantSetInfo, Verification};
use Conceptlz\AdobeSign\Enum\SignatureLevel;

ParticipantSetInfo::signer(
    ParticipantInfo::email('buyer@example.com')->verifyWith(Verification::emailOtp()),  // who may open it
)->signatureLevel(SignatureLevel::AES);                                                 // how they sign

    The methods and their trade-offs are covered in Identity verification.

    Common questions

    Is a signature with Email OTP an AES?

    No. It is still an SES. The one-time code proves that the person who opened the agreement controlled the email inbox at that moment, which strengthens your evidence, but it doesn't give the signer a certificate or bind the signature cryptographically to them. Ask for SignatureLevel::AES if you need an advanced signature; you can keep the OTP as well.

    Why did I see a provider dropdown when I signed?

    Because the agreement asked for a digital signature (AES or QES), either through the client default, AgreementInfo::signatureLevel() or the signer's own signatureLevel(). Acrobat Sign then lists the cloud signature providers enabled on the account, and the signer picks the one they hold a certificate with. If the list is empty or has the wrong providers, that is an account setting in Acrobat Sign admin.

    What's the difference between AES and QES if both use a provider?

    The certificate and the device. A QES uses a qualified certificate from a provider on an EU trusted list, after identity proofing to qualified standards, and the key lives on a certified QSCD. An AES can use any certificate that meets the four advanced requirements. The API request is identical, so the provider (and the specific service it offers) decides the outcome. Check it with signatureEvidence() and your qualified-issuers list.

    Can one signer be AES and another SES?

    Yes. Levels are set per participant set: call signatureLevel() on the ParticipantSetInfo that needs a different level. It overrides the agreement's level in either direction. After signing, check each signer against their own requirement, because meets() applies one level to everyone (see Checking a mixed agreement).

    Is a simple electronic signature legally binding?

    In the EU and the UAE, an electronic signature cannot be rejected just because it is electronic, so an SES can bind the parties to most commercial contracts. Its weakness is evidential: if the signer denies signing, you have to prove it, which is where the audit report and identity checks help. Some documents need a higher level or are excluded from e-signing altogether; ask counsel.

    Why does the evidence say TYPED for a digital signature?

    signatureInputType describes the visible appearance the signer chose, and Adobe reported TYPED for a certificate-based signature in live testing. Look at $signer->digitallySigned and $signer->certificateIssuer instead.

    Can the SDK prove that a signature is qualified?

    It can tell you that a signature was digital and which CA issued the certificate, and it compares that issuer with the list you provide. It cannot check the trusted list itself. For a formal answer, validate the signed PDF with trusted-list-aware validation software.

    If I change the project default to AES, what happens to agreements already sent?

    Nothing. The level is part of the request that creates the agreement; changing ClientOptions later affects only new agreements built with AgreementInfo. To change an agreement in flight, cancel it and send a new one (see Managing agreements).
